### Community

- **One Liner:** A 2015 hybrid from Defiant Development in which you play through randomly dealt card-based dungeon quests against a talking Dealer, with real-time third-person action combat whenever a fight card turns up.
- **Consensus Summary:** Hand of Fate is widely regarded as a distinctive, charming genre mash-up that predates Slay the Spire and helped seed the roguelike deckbuilder space. Steam players rate it Very Positive (about 89% of roughly 10,700 reviews), and critics landed at a Metacritic 78 on PC (79 PS4, 80 Xbox One), with user score 7.9. The Dealer, the card-driven storytelling and the unlock loop are loved; the simple Arkham-style combat, heavy RNG, repetition and a harsh final boss are the standing complaints. Many current players recommend it mainly on sale and say the sequel is the better entry, though some prefer the original's looser deckbuilding and flavor. It is a finished game (studio closed in 2019) and is not early access.
- **Reception Notes:** Launched February 2015 to generally favorable reviews (Metacritic PC 78 from 28 critics; user score 7.9 from 213 ratings) praising the concept and Dealer while noting slow pacing, repetitive combat and a hard final level. Wikipedia cites about 400,000 copies sold across platforms plus over 1.5 million downloads from an Xbox Live giveaway; SteamSpy lists 500k-1M Steam owners and the steam-revenue-calculator Boxleiter-style estimate is about $5.7M gross and $1.7M net on Steam (order-of-magnitude only; no copies figure given). Released on Windows, macOS, Linux, PS4 and Xbox One; a Vita version was cancelled. Hand of Fate 2 (Nov 2017) improved on it and outsold it over comparable periods. Defiant Development ceased operations in July 2019. Today the game has a low player count on Steam (single-digit concurrent players in our data) but remains well regarded; recent reviews commonly say to buy it on sale. #### Best Things

- The Dealer: a menacing, talkative, witty narrator whose voice acting and banter are the glue of the game and the most-praised element
- Original concept: a physical-feeling card table that generates the dungeon, events and shops, with the cards acting as the storytelling layer
- Unlock loop: completing encounters awards tokens that unlock new equipment and encounter cards, so each run feels like it grows your deck and gives a sense of progress
- Risk/reward encounters, where choices, dice, and gambles on the board can pay off or punish, and failure still yields unlocks
- Atmosphere and presentation: beautiful card art, dark-fantasy tone, and a thematic soundtrack that reviewers called strong for an indie budget
- Over-the-top, satisfying combat effects and slow-motion finishers for players who like a simple action layer
- Accessible deckbuilding that lets you stuff in newly found cards and experiment without heavy optimization
- Good value and a long tail for completionists (users report 20-190 hours chasing unlocks, endless mode and achievements)

#### Worst Things

- Combat is simplistic, Arkham-style button mashing with little depth, and many reviewers called it clunky, floaty or repetitive
- Camera control is restrictive; players can be stuck with an angle that hides off-screen attacks and traps, both in combat and on the board
- Heavy RNG: whether you assemble a workable kit before the boss can feel out of your control, and one reviewer alleges failed quests resurface more often
- The final boss and final level are a sharp difficulty spike, described as a boss rush and unfair by several reviewers, and some never finished
- Limited variety of encounters and enemies means the same fights and events repeat quickly
- Jank and unpolished balance: unfixed bugs, inconsistent mouse/controller handling, endless-mode glitches, and uninterruptible attacks not clearly telegraphed
- The Dealer's long lines and the slow card dealing animation grate on repeat runs
- Marketing mismatch: several reviewers say it is an action game with cards as a storytelling layer, not a real card game or deckbuilder, so the roguelike-deckbuilder label disappoints

#### Improvements

- Make combat deeper with better telegraphs and readable distinction between interruptible and non-interruptible attacks
- Add camera control, or at least a better fixed angle in combat and on the board
- Give the player more agency over builds and RNG, with a smoother difficulty curve into the final boss
- Allow skipping or speeding up the initial deal and repeated Dealer dialogue
- More encounter and enemy variety, and a more meaningful post-campaign/endless mode beyond repeating runs for score
- Clean up the achievement list, which players say is bloated with build-specific or pure-luck entries

#### Unique Things

- Two decks: one for equipment and one for the encounters that define the dungeon, so you literally choose the events you might face
- A fusion of tabletop-style board progression with real-time action RPG combat, rare in its period
- A personified adversary Dealer who narrates, taunts and reacts to your luck as the game master
- Cards that both unlock and chain into follow-up encounters, weaving side quests into the main campaign through the deck
- Predates Slay the Spire by about two years yet plays very differently from later roguelike deckbuilders, since cards are narrative and loadout rather than a combat hand
- Spawned a physical companion board game (Hand of Fate: Ordeals) and a stronger sequel, and a new spin-off in the same universe is reported in development

#### Tedious Or Disliked

- Repeating the same encounters several times, sometimes with specific choices, to unlock certain upgrades
- Early-game slog: several reviewers found the first half slow before it becomes engaging
- Mashing through waves of swarming enemies, which feels repetitive and mindless to some
- Endless mode after unlocking everything reduces to replaying similar runs for a better score
- Long Dealer monologues and slow dealing animations that cannot be skipped easily
- Grinding through RNG-dependent runs to land a viable build and non-crippling curses before the final boss

#### Divisive Points

- Combat: some enjoy the flashy, simple brawling as a fun counterpoint to the cards, while others call it the weakest part and overshadowing everything else
- Dealer: loved by most as a charismatic villain, but a vocal minority find him an annoying over-talker
- Deckbuilding: fans like its freedom and low barrier versus sequel's stricter approach, while critics call it too limited; some also object that it is not really a card game despite the marketing
- Original vs sequel: many say Hand of Fate 2 is strictly better, others argue the first has more flavor and an easier, more brainless style they prefer
