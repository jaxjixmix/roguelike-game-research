- **Summary:** Alina of the Arena is a deck-building roguelike where cards are used to move and attack on a tactical board, blending Slay the Spire style deckbuilding with Into the Breach style positioning. He discovered it through a stream viewer's recommendation and fell in love with it after 10-15 hours. He feels the board gimmick is strong enough to stop it from being a Slay the Spire clone while staying close to its familiar structure. He places it in A tier and thinks it could grow with more content.
- **Quote:** I kind of fell in love with it
- **Rerank Note:**

##### Praise

- A unique board-movement mechanic gives the game its own identity while keeping Slay the Spire familiarity
- Cards are well designed and support many different builds
- Each character starts with a unique deck and abilities, so runs feel different
- Three floors that each look visually different give a sense of progression
- Bosses have unique mechanics and encounter variety avoids repetition
- A surprising story twist at the end and fun lore
- Content looks easy to extend with new cards, characters and bosses

##### Criticism

- Balance is off: movement-related cards are extremely powerful and can carry a run
- Fights can run long, especially with defensive builds
- Fewer cards and builds than Slay the Spire; builds may converge over many hours
- No unique card pools per character, only unique starting decks

##### Unique Points

- The board and movement mechanic is the gimmick that differentiates it from Slay the Spire clones
- He argues modern deckbuilders need a unique mechanic or they feel like clones
- Keeps near-identical cards and keywords to Slay the Spire while designing around the board

##### Comparisons

- Slay the Spire
- Into the Breach

##### Design Criteria

- Unique mechanic/gimmick: must differentiate from Slay the Spire clones
- Build diversity: many builds but fewer than Slay the Spire
- Character variety: unique starting decks and abilities per character
- Progression and level visuals: three visually distinct floors
- Enemy/boss design: unique boss mechanics and varied encounters
- Balance: movement is overpowered
- Run/fight length: long fights, especially defensive builds
- Content expandability: easy to add cards, characters, bosses

#### Best Things

- Hex movement is central: pushing enemies, dodging and positioning make fights feel like tactical puzzles, which reviewers say gives it more depth than Slay the Spire's simple block
- Dual-wield equipment system (daggers, sword and shield, two-handed weapons) with card colours tied to each hand adds build identity
- Soundtrack widely praised, with the Act 2 boss track singled out by a 100 percent completion reviewer
- Pixel art, crunchy sound effects and animations feel polished and run smoothly, including on controller
- Balanced and challenging but fair; difficulty can be set per run without losing unlock progress, and high difficulty and max-stage challenges extend play
- Card synergies and cursed weapons enable satisfying combo turns and occasionally broken runs
- Free demo, and a low price (often about 7 USD on sale) that reviewers call good value
- Free-action tossing of rocks and dropped weapons from the ground is an appreciated small tactical layer

#### Worst Things

- Card and effect descriptions are vague (burn, focus, stacking wounds), so you cannot make informed decisions and regret picks
- Limited content: small card pool, few bosses (about 7 major), and build-defining cards become obvious within a few runs
- Classes are similar, sharing card pools with thin differences; starting class Strike/Defend cards are just renamed with the class name
- Event encounters are text only, often just trade money for an item, with no illustrations or aftermath images
- Almost no story or worldbuilding until the ending
- Console reviewers cite awkward controls, weak UI and a small dim sprites problem on Switch; no touch support
- Some players call the developer inactive and the game effectively abandoned; Steam Workshop is listed but content is thin

#### Improvements

- Write precise, numeric card and status text (for example deals 1 per stack per turn)
- Add illustrated events with outcomes and a bit more narrative framing, such as intros or boss VS screens
- Give each class distinct starting cards and names, and widen the card pool and viable builds
- Add more content: bosses, enemy variety, arenas (there is only one board type) and more Workshop/mod content
- Improve tutorial coverage and onboarding for the colour-switching and movement rules
- Rebalance weapons (katana reported strong, ranged and heavy weapons weak) and make higher difficulties add mechanics, not just stat bumps

#### Unique Things

- Explicit fusion of deckbuilding with Into the Breach-style tactical grid movement, including knockbacks and dodges instead of simple attack/defend
- Hand-coloured cards: equipment in left and right hands and colour switching shape what cards do
- Classes styled like Dark Souls starting builds (Pyromancer, Deprived, Slave and others), encouraging themed builds
- Dropping and throwing ground objects as a free action
- Static arena with no map RNG, so all variation comes from cards, enemies and events
- Per-run difficulty selection that keeps unlock progress

#### Tedious Or Disliked

- Long runs: normal runs reportedly take 1 to 1.5 hours and some up to 2 hours
- Early game can be punishing because you start with few movement cards and depend on drawing them
- Colour-switching and control schemes are confusing for newcomers, especially on a gamepad
- Repeated fights and bosses feel repetitive after the first several runs

#### Divisive Points

- Build variety: some love the focused class identities; others find classes too limiting versus Slay the Spire
- Difficulty: some call it brutal but rewarding, others call combat easy; also whether Veteran-level scaling is interesting
- Value: praised as a bargain on sale but one reviewer rejected the roughly 12.49 EUR price as too short and repetitive
- Whether the thin story and event presentation matters, given the fast gameplay loop
