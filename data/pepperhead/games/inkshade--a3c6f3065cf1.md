##### Praise

- Out-of-combat vibe: a house that slowly opens up, with a narrator who guides and sometimes misleads you

- Heavily dependent on meta-progression stat upgrades, so some fights are mathematically unwinnable without them

- No real builds during a run; fights are predictable from start to end and the same board repeats
- Ending was less satisfying than hoped; low replayability as a one-time adventure

- Copies Inscryption's vibe without its card gameplay, which is harder to clone than typical mechanics
- Only two damage and defense types (magical or physical)
- Acts more like a one-time adventure than a replayable roguelike

#### Best Things

- Atmosphere, art direction, lighting and sound design (the wooden 'bonk' of pieces, battle themes, end-credits track) are the most consistently praised element
- The game master is a memorable, sardonic and malicious narrator whose worldbuilding and banter give the run a sense of story
- Escape-room style puzzle sections in locked rooms give a satisfying break from combat and several reviewers found them clever and tough
- Tactical board is simple and readable: pieces cannot pass through each other, so bottlenecks, shielding and elevation matter
- Collecting and upgrading new pieces feels rewarding, and tense battles where you could lose your whole roster give real dread
- Strong Inscryption-like vibe with different mechanics (mid-run recruiting of defeated enemies, hex-grid tactics) that some find a fresh spin
- Steam Deck works well per at least one reviewer (minor remapping), plus accessibility options like adjustable difficulty and colour alternatives

#### Worst Things

- Tactical depth is shallow: units are differentiated almost entirely by five stats (movement, health, range, damage, defense) and only a few have special abilities
- Balance is exploitable: stacking armour/attack on one or two pieces (the Wretch is repeatedly named) makes most enemies and bosses trivial, so strategy falls away
- Enemy AI is passive and only engages when a piece is in range, enabling simple luring tactics
- Very dark visuals; even with lantern and brightness options, some players cannot see enemy units and felt unfairly killed
- Performance and stability complaints: low fps on mid-range GPUs, some maps heavy, and one reviewer reported frequent crashes on an older Win10 PC (fine on a newer machine)
- Controls can be clumsy: passing a unit's turn with a triple click is easy to trigger accidentally, no clear attack-range preview for ranged units before moving, unit names hard to see
- The narrator talks a lot and dialogue cannot easily be skipped even after many repeats
- Story and character connections are thin and the ending left some players unsatisfied; limited replayability after finishing

#### Improvements

- Add more unit abilities and build variety (area attacks, taunt, summons, status effects) so pieces differ beyond stat numbers
- Rebalance upgrade costs and scaling so one maxed piece or armour stacking cannot trivialise the game, and so early grinding feels less punishing
- Improve brightness/visibility and add camera options (decouple camera from mouse, manual camera toggle) to address eye strain and motion sickness
- Fix the unit-pass input (allow un-passing or use a less accident-prone control) and show attack range and unit names more clearly
- Let players skip or shorten repeated narrator dialogue
- Optimise performance and stability, and consider modding/community map support or more backstory content (suggested by one reviewer)

#### Unique Things

- Combines first-person escape-room exploration of locked rooms and an airship setting with a turn-based tactics board game run by a hostile game master
- Pieces are wooden miniatures on a physical-feeling board with hex grid, elevation and height effects
- Roguelite meta-progression through spending scrip on permanent upgrades to a collected roster, plus a mid-run recruitment system for defeated enemies
- A post-campaign challenge mode (including a Monomanipulation mode for single-unit runs added in Sep 2025) that players say is closer to a classic roguelite
- Unlocking new pieces is tied to solving puzzle rooms, and opening rooms is tied to progressing through battles

#### Tedious Or Disliked

- Grind: you need upgrades to win fights and need to fight (and often lose) to earn upgrade currency; reviewers cite only about 15-20 scrip per failed run versus 50 for a single point of damage
- Dying can send you back large sections, keeping pieces and upgrades but losing run progress, which feels heavy in mid/late game
- Enemies and early encounters scale with progress, so knowledge and skill matter less than how many upgrades you bought
- Spreading upgrades across many pieces is a trap; new players may have to restart or soft-lock themselves on harder settings after a reset
- Long runs with repetitive combat and a chatty narrator during tutorial/first-hour failure scripting
- Darkness and camera behaviour causing eye strain or motion sickness for some

#### Divisive Points

- Inscryption comparison: some see a worthy atmospheric cousin, others call it a shallow imitation without Inscryption's mechanical depth
- Difficulty: some enjoy brutal early hours and tense fights, others find it grindy early and then trivial once an overpowered build is found
- Puzzle rooms: praised as a highlight by some, called shallow or unclear by others
- Narrator and story: charming and creepy to some, repetitive and flat to others
