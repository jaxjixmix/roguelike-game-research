- **Summary:** SpellRogue is a dice-based deckbuilding roguelike where spells trigger on specific dice rolls. PepperHead likes the art style and finds the randomness less punishing than in most games of its genre. His main problem is that the roguelike layer is thin: only about five or six spell slots, limited upgrades, few passives and events, and almost no change in build from one floor to the next. He finds runs slow and repetitive, and he got bored quickly. He thinks it could improve with more content and places it in C tier.

- **Rerank Note:**

##### Praise

- Appealing art style
- Handles dice randomness better than most deckbuilding dice games; low rolls are still usable
- Spells keyed to specific roll values let you build a deck that covers low and high rolls
- Core mechanics are pretty cool
- Has room to grow with future updates

##### Criticism

- Very few spell slots and all spells are found quickly
- Spells can be upgraded only once and upgrades are often not very impactful
- Few passives, events and unique things to find during a run
- Slow pacing and repetitive fights; build barely changes by the third floor
- No permanent roguelite meta-progression, so every run feels the same
- Only about three characters, with starting decks that must be unlocked
- Dice randomness still favors high rolls (sixes) and undercuts the strategy side

##### Unique Points

- Spells activate on particular dice values, so deck building means covering low and high rolls
- Many spells scale with how high the rolled value is

##### Comparisons

- Astria (Ascending)
- other deckbuilding dice games

##### Design Criteria

- Randomness vs strategy: dice luck in a turn-based strategy game erodes strategic depth
- Build variety: few spell slots and quick convergence limit choices
- Upgrade depth: single, weak upgrades
- Run pacing: slow and repetitive within a run
- Meta-progression: lacks permanent unlocks to make runs feel different
- Content variety: few events, passives and characters
- Visuals: art style is praised

#### Best Things

- Dice-driven spell casting keeps the variance of a card game but gives players more agency and skill expression; many compare it favourably to Slay the Spire
- Huge build variety: 350+ spells with branching dual upgrades, shards/badges, 145+ artifacts, and several viable archetypes per character
- Four elemental characters (fire, earth, water, wind) with their own mechanics and multiple starting loadouts
- UI and information display are praised as adaptive and clear, and the game plays well on mouse, keyboard and controller
- Runs are fairly short and do not overstay their welcome; some reviewers find the pacing of progression and achievements satisfying
- Strong art direction (board-game-like tiled maps and figurine characters), good graphics and card/spell design
- Long-term replay from daily challenges, the Descent difficulty ladder, mutators and meta-progression unlocks
- Good value on sale; a demo with carried-over progress was appreciated

#### Worst Things

- Meta-progression and unlocking feel grindy, with some calling the unlock structure bloat rather than meaningful new content
- Higher Descent/difficulty tiers are described as a slog, with semi-random modifiers undermining build experimentation
- Combat rules are hard to grasp at first, and some players find few synergy paths so building up power over a run is difficult (the most-upvoted negative review)
- Balance: some enemies and forced debuffs hard-counter builds, offered spells are often weak, and scaling is hard to come by in some runs
- Late-game and some runs feel luck-dependent; a few players say you can tell after two fights whether a run is viable
- Repetitive enemy variety and a feeling that you have seen everything the game offers after a while
- Combat screens carry a lot of effects and information, which is overwhelming and hard to parse at first
- Audio/SFX mixing criticised by some; Steam Deck battery drain and performance mentioned in one review
- Some reviewers find it competent but unremarkable (a '5/10') and say it lacks the choice impact of Slay the Spire

#### Improvements

- Offer clearer onboarding for the combat rules and more reliable ways to build synergies and scaling
- Add more enemy variety and more varied encounter/path design
- Allow swapping spells in and out before a fight for more flexibility
- Make spell and artifact offers less often useless, and strengthen scaling options such as runes
- Reduce reliance on grinding for unlocks and rework difficulty modifiers so they are less punishing
- Improve the audio mix and polish remaining UI/icon rough edges and controller dice-selection bugs

#### Unique Things

- Not a draw-pile deck: you equip up to 7 spells on the field with extra in reserve, and power them with dice
- Mana dice can be rerolled, duplicated, split and enchanted, turning dice manipulation into a build axis
- Each spell has branching upgrade paths plus shards and badges, giving layered customisation
- Enemies actively disrupt spells and dice (curses, ignite and spreading debuffs), so you are managing your own toolkit as well as the enemy
- Board-game presentation with tiled maps and figurine-like characters
- Descent difficulty system and mutators for escalating challenge

#### Tedious Or Disliked

- Grinding character levels and unlocks, called out by the Gaming Age critic and several Steam reviewers
- High-difficulty late game repeated runs described as a horrific slog
- Friction of rolling dice and placing them into spell slots every turn wore some players down over many runs
- Lots of on-screen effect and information tracking each turn
- Runs with weak starting loadouts or useless offers feel like you should just restart

#### Divisive Points

- Dice luck versus control: fans say dice manipulation gives consistency, while others say you mostly win when lucky
- Difficulty: some find it too easy early yet dragged out, while others call it dull and punishing
- Meta-progression: praised as a deep well of content by some, dismissed as padding or a grind by others
- Versus Slay the Spire: some say it improves on StS, others say the devs did not grasp what made StS great
