### Commentary


#### Entry 1

- **Part:** 5
- **Label:** Tier list Part 5 (2026)
- **Summary:** Mewgenics is Edmund McMillan's follow-up to The Binding of Isaac: a board-centric, turn-based strategy roguelike where you command a squad of cats with very different play styles. He treats it as deserving its hype, with enormous content (100+ hours and he still hasn't hit the true ending), endlessly varied fights, and runs that feel fresh every time. He still has gripes about breeding opacity, shops and equipment restrictions, but they matter less after 100 hours. He calls it an easy S tier.
- **Quote:** I think Mugenics is an easy S tier. No question.
- **Rerank Note:** Re-evaluation: he previously made an in-depth review at about 30 hours; now at about 100 hours his early complaints still apply but bother him less.

##### Praise

- Huge amount of content, arguably more than any other roguelike including Isaac, for the price
- Every board looks different thanks to the many abilities, items and enemies, like shuffling a deck of cards
- Strategic depth rewarded by knowledge, with relatively little RNG so skilled players win most runs confidently
- Game-breaking combos are hard to earn, so being overpowered feels rewarding rather than trivial
- Losing cats does not feel terrible since new ones are easy to get and best cats only go on adventures once
- Pre-run setup (picking cats, roles, items) takes 10+ minutes and makes the start of each run unique
- Long main quest, many side quests and meta progression that unlocks options rather than raw stat boosts

##### Criticism

- Breeding system is complicated and confusing, and takes too long to teach the player how it works
- Equipment placement limits and curses can lock a cool item away from a character
- Questionable quality of life such as pathing that sends cats through spikes
- Choice events should let you pick which cat (or between two cats) handles them
- In-run shops are boring, mostly just buying level-ups, so money feels less exciting than in Isaac
- Instant-death or run-ruining effects like charm can feel unfair, though they have been reduced

##### Unique Points

- A completely different genre (turn-based tactics board) from Isaac yet with similar content density and knowledge-driven design
- Pre-run team building solves the common roguelike problem of repetitive run openings
- Permanent cat loss is softened by breeding and easy replacement, unlike punishing losses in Darkest Dungeon
- Meta progression is about breeding systems and options, not plain stat upgrades

##### Comparisons

- The Binding of Isaac
- Darkest Dungeon
- Cubic Cosmos
- Enter the Dungeon

##### Design Criteria

- Content volume: hundreds of hours with a long main quest and side quests
- Gameplay variety: abilities, items and enemies combine so each board differs
- Strategy and knowledge: skill and game knowledge beat RNG
- Risk vs reward: overpowered builds are earned, and losses are softened
- Run start variety: extensive customization before each run
- Meta progression: unlocks options and breeding rather than raw power, but slow
- Quality of life: pathing, event cat choice and equipment limits
- Fairness: instant-death and charm effects can ruin runs
- Economy: shops and money feel less meaningful than in Isaac
- Onboarding: breeding information arrives too slowly

#### Best Things

- Extremely deep tactical combat with 1,000+ abilities and 900+ items, producing wild synergies and build variety; players compare it to Final Fantasy Tactics crossed with Magic-style drafting and combo-building
- The cat breeding and genetics layer (mutations, birth defects, inherited traits) creates emergent stories and strange, hilarious builds
- Soundtrack widely singled out as a highlight, in some Steam reviews the best part of the game
- Huge content volume: 200+ hour campaign, 100+ bosses, many unique enemies that each demand a specific plan; reviewers with 60+ hours say they had seen under a third of it
- Strong Edmund McMillen personality: grotesque humour, charming art style, and a true Binding-of-Isaac-style sense of discovery
- Highly addictive loop; multiple reviewers describe ragequitting then sinking dozens of hours anyway
- Acts 1 and 2 are frequently described as engaging and easy to jump back into

#### Worst Things

- House and cat management is clunky: no way to sort or filter cats by stat, no simple way to clear accumulating poop, and extra rooms unlock very late
- UI problems in battle: easy to misclick, cannot see other cats' items and abilities mid-fight when it is not their turn, and ability descriptions are inconsistent or omit ranges
- Act 3 is a difficulty spike; several reviewers say runs turn stressful rather than fun, and losing a run can mean losing cats you spent a long time breeding
- Hidden or unclear information forces knowledge checks in a turn-based game, which some find fair in Isaac but annoying in a tactics game
- Some events offer a trivial upside against severe downsides (for example money versus injuring a cat), which reads as unfair
- Humour does not land for everyone: a KonsoliFIN critic called it often grating and dated, and another reviewer called it criminally unfunny
- Display options at launch: no borderless fullscreen and no 16:10 aspect ratio support, per a popular positive Steam review
- Heavy RNG can frustrate players wanting a purely skill-based challenge (noted in critic consensus)
- Drafting abilities without being told what you are choosing between or what they do in context, which several top negative reviews cite as poor onboarding for TBS players

#### Improvements

- Add sorting, filtering, labeling or renaming tools for cats and a faster way to handle the house (breaking poop, bulk actions)
- Streamline the post-run breeding/management loop so it takes less time between runs
- Improve battle UI clarity: safer click targets, view all allies' abilities and items on any turn, consistent ability text with stated ranges
- Let players preserve or test more items or builds so late-game is not fully at the mercy of roguelike drops and stockpiling broken items
- Smooth Act 3 balance and reduce the feeling that hidden content is gated behind borderline annoying runs
- Better display support (borderless fullscreen, more aspect ratios); the developers have since added four languages and console ports

#### Unique Things

- Fuses Isaac-style chaotic item roguelite design with a FFT-like tactics grid, a deck-building-flavored ability draft, and a genetic breeding sim
- Permanent cat lineages carry traits, mutations and defects between runs, so your breeding house is effectively the meta-progression
- Every cat has a voice and personality, and disorders, parasites and injuries are mechanical features with absurd effects (for example a corpse-eating trait that also eats teammates)
- Ridiculon soundtrack and performances tie into the marketing, with live Gamescom performance
- Long gestation: announced in 2012 as a Super Meat Boy follow-up, cancelled, then rebooted in 2018 with Tyler Glaiel into the current tactics design

#### Tedious Or Disliked

- Managing a growing population of cats in the house: clicking through every cat to compare stats, with no list or filter
- Runs that last an hour or more, then a long management step after each run
- Stockpiling items to take into later runs, which some see as a cop-out to justify harder late enemies
- Grindiness noted by several reviewers, especially chasing unlocks and finishing the later acts
- Losing a run late can erase many hours of breeding progress, which makes Act 3 feel stressful
- Sorting which items to keep after each run via a large grid with very limited filtering

#### Divisive Points

- Humour and tone: some call it the heart of the game, others find the crude and dated jokes grating
- Difficulty and chaos over balance in Act 3: some love breaking the game with synergies, others find the required game-break every run unfun
- Breeding system changes in patch 1.1 (stimulation, inbreeding, mutation tweaks): intended as balance and depth but some players say it made management more of a pain
- Amount of hidden or discoverable information: praised as Isaac-like discovery by some, criticised as bad UX for a tactics game by others
